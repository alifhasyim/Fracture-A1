\section{Introduction} 
\hspace{2em}In this first assignments, the main focus will be on performing crack analysis
using finite element method (FEM) in Abaqus software using two approaches. The first
method is to perform a pre-defined crack analysis without separation between two materials,
whereas the area around the crack tip will be evaluated to obtain the behavior of the stress field. Hence, J-integral
will be calculated based on the stress field using domain integral method to obtain the energy release quantity. The key takeaway from this
is to find the stable value of J-integral based on the contour integral surrounding the crack, which will be used
as a reference value for the crack initiation value.

\hspace{2em}The second task is to analyze crack propagation using the cohesive zone method. Hence, some
cohesive elements will be defined along the pre-defined crack path with the 
cohesive parameters with an initial crack opening on the front of the cohesive line. 
From the task definition, the separation behavior of the cohesive are will be observed, as well as the energy release 
rate. This can be achieved by plotting the traction-separation curve, which will show the
relationship between the traction and separation displacement along the cohesive zone. Another key aspect that will be obtained
is to know the force-time response during the crack propagation, so that it can be correlated with the traction-separation
curve.

\hspace{2em}Both figures above shows the stress field for the plane stress and strain condition for figure~\ref{fig:Q1_Elastic_PStress} and~\ref{fig:Q1_Elastic_PStrain}
respectively. In plane stress, the Von-Misses stress area as well as the value are larger compare to the plane strain. The area shows more rounded shape
with an almost equal radius, while the plane strain created a larger stress distribution near the back tip of the crack.

\hspace{2em}This difference is caused by the different set-up and definition of plane stress and strain model. Where in the plane stress, the out-plane stress is essentially zero:

\begin{equation}
  \sigma_z = 0
\end{equation}

\hspace{2em} Whereas in the plane strain, the constraint is located in the strain out-of-plane is defined as
follows:

\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_z = 0
\end{equation}

\hspace{2em}Theoretically, as the strain is constraint in the out-plane direction, additional stress will be generated due to the restriction of
movement in one plane. However, the main context here is the Von-Misses stress, which basically dependant to the deviatoric part of the stress instead of 
the hydrostatic part. Hence, the basic Von-Misses formulation is calculated as follows:

\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}s_{ij}s{ij}}
\end{equation}

\hspace{2em}Where the deviatoric shear component can be described using the hydrostatic term $\sigma_h$ in following equation:

\begin{equation}
  s_{ij} = \sigma_{ij} - \sigma_h \delta_{ij}
\end{equation}

\hspace{2em}As mentioned before, the plane strain condition will increase the triaxiality stress component ($\eta = \frac{\sigma_h}{\sigma_{vm}}$), by means of increasing the hydrostatic stress due to 
restriction of one axis freedom, consequently generating additional stress in that direction. Hence, the shear component consequently decreased as well as the overall Von-Misses stress. While in plane stress, the shear component
will be larger compare to plane strain as it allows more freedom in the out-plane movement, which therefore increase the Von-Misses value. To visualize the stress distribution
numerically, figure~\ref{fig:Q1_Elastic_Stress_Curve} shows the Von-Misses distribution along path C, which describe additionally in the graph picture.

\hspace{1em}In this section, J-integral values are evaluated for both elastic and elastic-plastic cases in plain stress
condition. To define the elastic-plastic behavior, Ramberg-Osgood flow curve is generated to describe the Plasticity
region in the flow curve. Ramberg-Osgood relation is defined as following equation

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon = \frac{\sigma}{E} + \alpha \left( \frac{\sigma}{\sigma_y} \right)^n
    \label{eq:RambergOsgood}
\end{equation}

\hspace{1em}Where $\varepsilon$ is the total strain, $\sigma$ is the stress, 
$E$ is the Young's modulus, $\sigma_y$ is the yield stress, n is the strain hardening exponent,
and $\alpha$ is the coefficient for controlling the yield curve transition. To generate the flow curve, equation
~\ref{eq:RambergOsgood} is rearranged to find the stress value based on the strain value, 
which is then plotted to obtain the flow curve as in figure~\ref{fig:RambergOsgood}. The modified equation, which the values are directly
inserted to Abaqus material parameters definition, can be written as follows:

\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \sigma_y \left( \frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha \varepsilon_y} \right)^{\frac{1}{n}}
    \label{eq:modifiedRambergOsgood}
\end{equation}

\hspace{1em}Important note that the yield stress is defined as 300 MPa based on table~\ref{tab:PlasticityQ1Q2}, hence the yield strain
can be calculated as follows:

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_y = \frac{\sigma_y}{E} = \frac{300 \text{ MPa}}{210000 \text{ MPa}} = 0.00142857
    \label{eq:yieldStrain}
\end{equation}

To validate the J-integral results, evaluating the theoretical J-integral value is conducted to determine the FEM accuracy. The first step is by knowing the stress intensity factor $K_I$ for the crack tip, 
which can be calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation}
    K_I = \frac{P}{B \sqrt{W}} f\left( \frac{a}{W} \right)
    \label{eq:StressIntensityFactor}
\end{equation}

Where P is the applied load, B is the thickness (considered as 1 mm for plane stress condition), W is the width of the plate,
and a is the crack length. The function $f\left( \frac{a}{W} \right)$ is a geometry correction factor, which can be calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation} 
  \begin{split} f\!\left(\frac{a}{W}\right) &
    = \frac{\,2 + \frac{a}{W}\,}{\left(1 - \frac{a}{W}\right)^{3/2}} 
    \Bigl( 0.886 + 4.64\frac{a}{W} - 13.32\left(\frac{a}{W}\right)^2 \\ 
    &\qquad + 14.72\left(\frac{a}{W}\right)^3 - 5.60\left(\frac{a}{W}\right)^4 \Bigr) 
  \end{split} 
  \label{eq:GeometryCorrectionFactor} 
\end{equation}

\hspace{1em}This equation is obtained based on literature Zhu et al. (2010) for compact C(T) specimen under mode I loading condition~\cite{Zhu.2012}. This is the closest
model that can be found, since both set up has similar test configuration, whereas in the literature, it used additional two holes in the geometry to introduce the concentrated force. Figure~\ref{fig:Q1_LiteratureGeom} shows the C(T) geometry with the geometry dimension
nomenclature details.


\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{images/Q1_LiteratureGeom.png}
  \caption{The compact tension (C(T)) specimen based on literature~\cite{Zhu.2012}. Two green arrows shows the applied concentration forces to introduce
  crack propagation.}
  \label{fig:Q1_LiteratureGeom}
\end{figure}

Another literature~\cite{Clarke.1979} mentioned by Clarke, that the geometry factor can be calculated using their proposed equation for
C(T) specimen:

\begin{equation}
  f\!\left(\frac{a}{W}\right) = 29.6(\frac{a}{W})^{0.5} - 185.5(\frac{a}{W})^{1.5} + 655.7(\frac{a}{W})^{2.5} - 1017(\frac{a}{W})^{3.5} + 638.9(\frac{a}{W})^{4.5} 
  \label{eq:ClarkeEq}
\end{equation}

\hspace{1em}Hence, the equation uses different factors to describe the relation of the ratio of the crack length and the 
specimen width. For $\frac{a}{W} = \frac{0.504}{2.4} = 0.21$ hence $f$\cite{Zhu.2012} = 4.401 using the equation~\ref{eq:GeometryFactor}. While using the equation~\ref{eq:ClarkeEq}
the results is $f$\cite{Clarke.1979} = 5.217. 

\hspace{1em}The last aspect that need to be addressed is the loading P. Whereas both literatures implemented concentrated forces applied in both gauge holes to place the loading, which shown in the green arrows in figure~\ref{fig:Q1_LiteratureGeom}. 
To obtain the equivalent load from the simulation,
the reaction force from the applied displacement is extracted from the FEM simulation by probing the reaction force in the y-direction
on the left edge of the plate. Based on the probing values in Abaqus, the reaction force is obtained as 205.559 N for the elastic case, while 
for elastic-plastic case the observed reaction force is 97.5102 N.

\hspace{1em}After the stress-intensity factor is obtained, the theoretical J-integral value can be calculated. Noted from the literature~\cite{Zhu.2012}, the J integral
is distinguished based on the material behavior. For elastic case, J-integral can be calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation}
    J_{el} = \frac{K_I^2}{E'}
    \label{eq:JIntegralElastic}
\end{equation}

Where $E'$ is the effective Young's modulus, which for plane stress condition is equal to E. Meanwhile, for elastic-plastic case, J-integral can be calculated using the following equation:
\begin{equation}
    J = J_{el} + J_{pl} = \frac{K_I^2}{E'} + \frac{\eta_{pl}A_{pl}}{Bb} 
    \label{eq:JIntegralElasticPlastic}
\end{equation}

Where $\eta_{pl}$ is a geometry factor, $A_{pl}$ is the plastic area under the load-displacement curve, $B$ is the thickness, and $b$ is the uncracked ligament length ($b = W - a$). 
For the plastic area based on the elastic-plastic force-displacement curve, the calculated area is 0.569 N.mm.


\hspace{1em}While the geometry factor can be calculated using referenced equation from Xian-Kui~\cite{Zhu.2012}, which can be described as following equation:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{pl} = 2 + 0.522 \frac{b}{W}
    \label{eq:GeometryFactor}
\end{equation}

\hspace{2em}To compute the force vs. time response, the reaction force ($R_{fy}$) in each node of the left upper edge
(where the displacement is applied) is probed in the y-direction. This will give values of reaction force for
every single mesh node, which then all of probed reaction force values are summed to obtain the total
reaction force. Abaqus has automatically plotted the total reaction force vs.time response. Therefore, the total force
can be calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation}
    R_{fy,total}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} R_{fy,i}(t)
    \label{eq:TotalReactionForce}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{images/Q2_ForceTimeResponse.png}
  \caption{Force-time response showing the total reaction force in the y-direction on the left edge of the upper part. The slope changes indicate crack initiation and propagation for stage 1 until stage 5.}
  \label{fig:Q2_ForceTimeResponse}
\end{figure}

\hspace{2em}To analyze the force behavior in the cohesive format, figure~\ref{fig:Q2_ForceTimeResponse} shows the plotted force-time response for the cohesive zone
model in a plane strain condition. 1st stage shows a constant stress distribution (this also can be refered in figure~\ref{fig:Q2_CrackPropagationPlot} from (a) to (b)), where the force
increases linearly with time. This step is also known as the pre-crack initiation, as the specimen remains
in the linear region. As the crack initiates (stage 2), the slope begins to decrease,
indicating the initial damage in the cohesive zone. This makes a decrease in the force value. As the
crack propagates further, stage 3 shows a minimum drop point of the force value, whereas the crack continues to propagate
(stage 4 - stage 5) the values remains relatively constant until the crack opening reach its maximum separation distance. 

\hspace{2em}During the 1st stage until the initial crack initialize, this region is known as a 
linear elastic region. Refer to the traction-separation curve in figure~\ref{fig:TractionSeparationCurve}, this region
is represented by the initial linear region until it reaches the peak nominal stress that is input in Abaqus user definition, which is 500 MPa.
As it reaches the peak nominal stress, the crack initiates and starts to propagate, which
is represented by the 2nd stage until the final stage in figure~\ref{fig:Q2_ForceTimeResponse}. The decrease in the slope 
force indicates that the cohesive zone is undergoing softening behavior, implied by the decrease of the load-bearing
capacity of the cohesive zone as the separation displacement increases. This will reach a minimum point, where the force is at the lowest state, shown is 3rd stage.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{images/Q2_CrackPlasticDeform.png}
  \caption{Plastic deformation around the crack tip during the crack propagation. The plastic deformation is observed
  to occur around the crack tip, indicating that the plate is undergoing plastic bending during the loading.}
  \label{fig:Q2_CrackPlasticDeform}
\end{figure}

\hspace{2em}As the crack propagates further, there is a slight increase in the force value, where it peaks located in the 4th
stage. These phenomena can be explained by the fact that the event is not purely contribute from the separation 
displacement in the normal direction, but also the plate plasticity plays a role during the deformation. As shown in preceeding figures, since the
plate has quite high aspect ratio (b/h = 1.5625), therefore the plate will have tendency to
undergo bending during the loading. Therefore, an increase in the force value is observed since the plastic-bending deformation
also contribute to the overall reaction force. After this local bending, the force is relatively constant until the crack reaches
its maximum separation displacement.
